Paying for it

The panels earn money, but the money that bought them isn't free. Either you're paying a loan every month, or you paid cash and gave up whatever that cash would have earned elsewhere. Both are a real monthly cost, and neither shows up in the electricity bill.

What this is. A planning model, not a quote and not advice. It answers "roughly, does this pay, and what moves the needle" — take it to an installer rather than instead of one, and check every tariff against your own contract before signing anything.

How it calculates. Everything is in today's shekels, so inflation is netted out of the return assumptions rather than inflating every figure. Production is the system size times the annual yield, falling by the degradation rate each year. A kWh you use yourself is worth the full retail tariff you avoid paying; a kWh you export is worth the feed-in tariff, plus the urban premium where it applies. Self-consumption is capped by what your household actually uses. The installed cost is the year-zero outflow; maintenance runs every year and the inverter is replaced once. Payback is where the cumulative position crosses zero, interpolated within the year. The return figure is the internal rate of return on that cash-flow series.

The big caveats. Tariffs are set by decision of the Electricity Authority and have moved repeatedly — the feed-in rate is locked for your contract term but the retail tariff it's compared against is not. Yield varies with roof orientation, tilt, shading and dust more than any single number suggests. Quotes differ in what they include: VAT, scaffolding, a new distribution board, structural work on an old roof. And self-consumption share, the single biggest lever here, is a guess unless you have interval data from your meter.
